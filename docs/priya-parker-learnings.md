# What Encore can learn from Priya Parker

Research notes on Priya Parker's *The Art of Gathering: How We Meet and Why It Matters* (Riverhead, 2018), her talks, her newsletter and her interviews, turned into product ideas for Encore.

**How this was researched.** Web search covered publisher pages, priyaparker.com and her newsletter, interview pages (NPR Life Kit, WNYC, Next Big Idea Club, The Motley Fool, PCMA, Brené Brown's *Unlocking Us*, The Ezra Klein Show) and reputable summaries. Direct page fetches were blocked in this environment (ted.com, npr.org and others did not resolve), so the summaries below rest on search-result excerpts plus the book's well-documented structure. Everything is paraphrased. Quoted fragments are only her short coined terms. Before anything is quoted in marketing copy, check the wording against the book.

**Lens for Encore.** Our hosts are friends running a dinner, game night, run club or supper club for about 4 to 20 people. Parker writes for every kind of gathering, including work ones. We keep the ideas that work in a group chat and a living room, and drop anything that needs a facilitator.

## Part 1 and 2: Principles and the features they suggest

Feature tags: **[Host]** or **[Guest]**. Each feature lists what the user sees, when it appears (before, at the door, during, closing, after) and why it delights.

---

### 1. Purpose over category

**Summary.** People plan by category ("a dinner party", "a birthday") and let habit decide the format. Parker says to start with why this gathering, with these people, right now. A good purpose is narrow enough that someone could disagree with it: her test is "specific, unique, disputable". Once you know the purpose, decisions about guests, place and format become easy.

**Sources:** PCMA, "Priya Parker: Don't Skip Over Purpose"; Motley Fool interview series (2018); Ezra Klein Show episode (Feb 2026), via Parker's Substack post; Brené Brown, *Unlocking Us*.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **"Why are we gathering?" line** [Host] | Event creation asks one playful required field after the title: "This is a ___ so that ___" with tap-to-fill examples ("a game night so that the new neighbors stop being strangers"). It shows as the subtitle on the invite. | Before (creation) | Takes ten seconds and makes the invite feel personal, not generic. It also gives every later feature something to work from. |
| **Purpose remix chips** [Host] | Under the category picker (Dinner / Game night / Run / Supper club), a few cards suggest sharper versions: "Dinner → Farewell dinner for Sam: one story each." | Before | Turns a bland category into a hook in one tap. Hosts feel clever without having to think hard. |
| **Purpose filter on discovery** [Guest] | Discovery cards lead with the purpose line, not just the date and place ("Sunday 7am run so that we all finally try the hill route"). | Before | Guests can tell at a glance whether the event is for them, which is more appealing than a list of venues. |

### 2. Exclude with purpose (and do it generously)

**Summary.** Parker argues that "the more the merrier" often weakens a gathering. Hosts should ask who fits and helps the purpose, who might undermine it, and who is only being invited out of obligation. She frames this as drawing a temporary line for the good of the guests who are there, and she coaches hosts through awkward messages like "Can I bring someone?"

**Sources:** priyaparker.com newsletter, "Why the More is Not Always the Merrier"; newsletter, "Three texts that haunt every host"; Motley Fool, "Who you don't invite is important"; Goodreads quotes page.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Guest-list sanity check** [Host] | While building the list, a gentle nudge appears: "12 people for a 'deep talk' dinner? 6–8 keeps it one conversation." It offers a "Save some for next time" list. | Before | Gives hosts permission to keep it small without guilt. The "next time" list turns leaving someone out into planning a future invite. |
| **Kind-reply templates for tricky texts** [Host] | When a guest taps "Ask to bring a +1", the host gets a reply sheet with warm one-tap answers ("This one's a tight table, but I'd love them at the next one!"). | Before | Removes the most dreaded hosting moment. Saying no stops feeling rude. |
| **Plus-one policy on the invite** [Guest] | A small, clear badge on the invite: "Just you this time" / "Bring a friend" / "Bring someone who's never been." | Before | Guests know where they stand and nobody gets an awkward exchange. "Bring someone new" is an invitation in itself. |

### 3. Choose the place, size and density on purpose

**Summary.** The setting shapes behavior, so pick a place that fits the purpose. A room slightly too small feels alive and the same crowd in a huge room feels flat. Parker gives rough sizes: around six people for one connected conversation, 8 to 12 for a lively dinner, 20 to 30 for a party. As a group thins out, people naturally gather in smaller spaces (the kitchen effect).

**Sources:** NPR Life Kit, "How to host a dinner party" (July 2021), with Parker; *The Art of Gathering*, chapter on place; book summaries (Shortform, Readingraphics).

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Group-size dial** [Host] | A capacity slider with emoji tiers and a one-line description of each: "4–6: one conversation", "8–12: buzzy table", "20+: party energy." | Before | Expert advice in a single tap. Hosts understand the trade-off instantly. |
| **Cozy-it-up tip** [Host] | On event day for groups that are small or shrinking (after cancellations): "Down to 5? Move to the kitchen table and dim the lights." | Before / during | Rescues the half-empty-room feeling at exactly the moment it happens. |

### 4. Don't be a chill host: use "generous authority"

**Summary.** Parker argues that the laid-back, hands-off host is often abdicating, and guests are left to fend for themselves. Her alternative is "generous authority": use the host's power for the guests' benefit. That means *protecting* them (rescuing someone cornered by a bore), *equalizing* them (stopping one voice or status from dominating) and *connecting* them (introductions don't happen on their own).

**Sources:** PCMA, "The New Era of Gathering: Embracing 'Generous Authority'"; WNYC *Brian Lehrer Show* segment; Motley Fool, "Don't be chill"; NPR Life Kit; Brené Brown, *Unlocking Us*.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intro matchmaker** [Host] | Once RSVPs are in, Encore suggests 2–3 introductions based on what guests shared ("Maya and Jon both just moved here. Introduce them early"). The host can send a pre-event "you two should meet" note. | Before / at the door | Makes the host look thoughtful. Guests arrive already knowing they have someone to talk to. |
| **Host pocket card** [Host] | A one-screen "tonight's game plan" with three short reminders (protect, equalize, connect) written as friendly nudges ("Who hasn't spoken yet?"). | During | A gentle confidence boost for nervous first-time hosts, without any corporate facilitation feel. |
| **Co-host role** [Host] | Assign a friend as "connector" or "door greeter" with a badge on the guest list. | Before / during | Shares the load and makes a friend feel trusted. |

---

### 5. Create a temporary alternative world with pop-up rules

**Summary.** Etiquette assumes everyone shares the same unspoken norms, and mixed groups often don't. Parker suggests explicit, playful "pop-up rules" that apply only for this gathering: no phones, no talking about work, everyone brings a dish from their grandmother's kitchen. Short-lived, clear rules let strangers relax into a shared "temporary alternative world" and give the night a personality.

**Sources:** priyaparker.com newsletter, "The Power of a Good Rule"; Motley Fool interview series; PCMA, "Reinventing the Rules for Gathering"; House of Beautiful Business, "A Temporary Alternative World for Those Who Enter It"; Tweetspeak Poetry book club, "Making and breaking rules".

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **House-rules deck** [Host] | Hosts pick 1–3 rules from playful cards ("Phones in the basket", "No shop talk", "Loser picks the next game", "Everyone runs the last km together") or write their own. They appear as stickers on the invite. | Before | The quickest way to give an event a personality. It's fun to choose and fun to read. |
| **Rule reveal on arrival** [Guest] | At check-in, the guest's screen flips to show tonight's rules with a small animation. | At the door | Marks the switch into "tonight's world" and gives everyone a shared joke from minute one. |

### 6. The gathering starts at the invitation (priming)

**Summary.** For Parker, an event begins the moment guests hear about it, not when the door opens. The name, the invitation's wording and small asks beforehand shape how people show up. Her point is that hosts spend too much effort preparing the food and too little preparing the people. A small pre-event prompt helps guests arrive already invested.

**Sources:** *The Art of Gathering*, chapter 5 summaries (SuperSummary, Shortform); Federal Judicial Center *In Session* episode 36 transcript (fjc.gov); NPR Life Kit.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One-question RSVP** [Guest] | RSVP includes one fun, host-chosen question ("A song that got you through this year?", "Your most controversial pizza opinion"). Answers are hidden until the event. | Before | Guests arrive curious and with something to say. The host gets conversation material for free. |
| **Name-the-night generator** [Host] | Suggests evocative names from the purpose ("The Bad Cookbook Club", "Hill Repeats & Pastries"). | Before | A good name makes an ordinary dinner feel like an occasion people talk about. |
| **Countdown nudges** [Guest] | 2 days before: a small "homework" note ("Bring one object with a story"). Day of: "See you at 7. Tonight's rule is no phones." | Before | Builds anticipation instead of the usual "reminder: event tomorrow" email. |

### 7. Mark the threshold

**Summary.** Arrival is a transition. Guests come in carrying their commute and their workday. Parker suggests a clear physical or symbolic doorway into the event (a greeting, a ritual, a moment that signals "you're here now") rather than letting people trickle into a vague in-between.

**Sources:** *The Art of Gathering* summaries (SuperSummary chapter 5; Shortform book overview); Next Big Idea Club conversation with Parker.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Welcome card on arrival** [Guest] | A "You're here!" screen when the guest taps "I've arrived" (or scans the host's QR code). It shows who's already there and a personal line from the host. | At the door | Turns the awkward walk-in into a warm moment. You immediately know who to look for. |
| **Doorway ritual picker** [Host] | Optional suggestions: "Greet everyone at the door", "Name tag with your RSVP answer", "Welcome drink with a toast at 7:15." | At the door | Small and doable, and it makes the first five minutes feel designed. |

---

### 8. Never start with logistics

**Summary.** Parker's chapter title "Never Start a Funeral with Logistics" makes the point: people remember beginnings, so don't waste yours on housekeeping and thank-yous. Open in a way that honors the guests and sets the tone, then slip the logistics in later. A strong opening tells people what kind of night this is.

**Sources:** *The Art of Gathering*, chapter 5 (SuperSummary; Shortform); Motley Fool, "1 big mistake we make".

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opener script** [Host] | A 3-line opening toast template drawn from the purpose line and the RSVP answers ("We're here because… Tonight there's one rule… Everyone brought a song, so let's hear one"). | At the door / during | Hosts have something to say that isn't "bathrooms are down the hall". It lowers stage fright. |
| **Logistics tucked away** [Guest] | Parking, Wi-Fi, door codes and cost splitting live behind an "Info" tab and never lead the invite. The invite leads with the purpose and the vibe. | Before | Invites read like an invitation, not a memo. |

---

### 9. Healthy controversy and real conversation

**Summary.** Parker argues that avoiding all friction makes gatherings boring. "Good controversy" is a structured, consented, contained way to let people say what they actually think, and it's often where the most memorable moments come from. Formats like a single shared conversation or themed toasts (her "15 Toasts" dinners, where each guest gives a short toast on a theme) give everyone a turn without making it heavy.

**Sources:** Motley Fool, "Have some good controversy"; Christian Century interview, "Intentional conflict"; House of Beautiful Business, "A Temporary Alternative World…" (15 Toasts); TED podcast and Freakonomics episodes with Parker.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spicy-question jar** [Host / Guest] | A tap-to-draw card deck with heat levels: Mild ("best meal of your life"), Medium ("overrated thing everyone loves"), Spicy ("a belief you changed your mind on"). The host sets the max heat. | During | Light structure that gets a table talking. The host-set heat level keeps it safe. |
| **Theme toasts mode** [Host] | Pick a theme ("firsts", "courage", "home"). Encore gives each RSVP a turn number and a gentle 1-minute timer. | During | A memorable group ritual with no prep. Everyone gets heard once. |

---

### 10. Help guests be seen and bring something of themselves

**Summary.** Parker repeatedly comes back to the idea that people leave remembering moments where they were seen and connected, not the canapés. Hosts can design small moments where guests share something real, and connection should be engineered rather than left to luck.

**Sources:** WNYC *Brian Lehrer Show*; NPR Life Kit (the host's job is helping everyone feel they belong); Next Big Idea Club conversation; Brené Brown, *Unlocking Us*.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reveal the RSVP answers** [Host] | During the event the host taps "Reveal" and everyone's pre-event answers appear as a fun group card or a guessing game ("Whose song is this?"). | During | Pays off the priming question. Instant laughter and guaranteed conversation starters. |
| **Regulars and firsts badges** [Guest] | Small markers on the guest list: "first time here", "5th supper club". | At the door / during | Helps hosts welcome newcomers and celebrate regulars, which is especially good for run clubs and recurring supper clubs. |

---

### 11. Close well: soft close, then inward and outward

**Summary.** Most gatherings fizzle out. Parker suggests a "soft close", like last call, so tired guests can leave gracefully, followed by a real ending. Her ending has two moves: "looking inward" (a moment to reflect on what happened together) and "turning outward" (preparing to go back to normal life). She advises not making the thank-yous and housekeeping the very last thing.

**Sources:** Tweetspeak Poetry book club, "When The Art of Gathering is Ending" (re-entry chapter); Inflexion, "Meaningful Endings Inward-Outward Activity" (an adaptation); Shortform summary; Goodreads reader notes.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Last-call nudge** [Host] | 30 minutes before the planned end, a quiet buzz: "Soft close? Announce last round / last game." | Closing | Ends the "do we leave now?" limbo. Guests can leave without awkwardness. |
| **One-word goodbye** [Guest] | The closing screen asks each guest for one word or one highlight from tonight. These collect into a shared "tonight in words" card. | Closing | A quick, warm group moment that gives the night a real ending, plus a keepsake. |

---

### 12. The after: re-entry and threads into the future

**Summary.** Parker treats the end as a bridge back to ordinary life. A gathering can leave threads behind: a shared memory, a commitment, a reason to meet again. Good hosts think about what guests carry out the door, not only what happens inside.

**Sources:** Tweetspeak Poetry, "Ending and re-entry"; *The Art of Gathering* closing chapter summaries (Shortform, Blinkist); Ezra Klein Show episode on recurring gatherings (Feb 2026).

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Morning-after recap** [Guest] | The next morning, a recap card arrives with the purpose line, the one-word goodbyes, the rules, who came, and a photo slot anyone can add to. | After | Brings the warmth back and is easy to share, which spreads Encore naturally. |
| **"Again?" one-tap** [Host] | The recap offers "Run it back": duplicate the event with the same people, rules and question, and pick a new date. | After | Turns a good night into a ritual, the core loop for run clubs and supper clubs. |
| **Thread forward** [Guest] | Guests can "pass the torch" by volunteering to host the next one ("I'll do the next game night"). | After | Spreads hosting among friends, which is exactly what keeps groups alive. |

---

### 13. The art of guesting

**Summary.** In her newsletter Parker turns the lens around: guests co-create the gathering too. A good guest commits to coming (no hedging), shows up fully, helps make meaning and thinks a little like a host. Being intentional about which invitations to accept is part of it.

**Sources:** priyaparker.com newsletter, "The Art of Guesting"; "The Art of Guesting During Festive Season"; Substack, "In defense of the skunk at the garden party"; WNYC *Brian Lehrer Show*.

| Feature | What the user sees | When | Why it's delightful |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No-maybe RSVP** [Guest] | RSVP buttons are "I'm in" / "Can't this time", with "Maybe" swapped for "Tell me by Thursday", which sets a gentle deadline. | Before | Hosts get real numbers. Guests feel the small pleasure of committing. |
| **"Bring something" slots** [Guest] | Guests claim a contribution (a dish, a playlist, a game, a question for the jar), shown on the invite. | Before | Gives guests a role, which increases investment and lightens the host's load. |
| **Guest gratitude note** [Guest] | After the event, a one-tap "thank the host" with a highlight from the closing words. | After | Hosts rarely get thanked specifically. This keeps them hosting. |

## Part 3: Top 6 features by delight per effort (shippable this week)

Ranked for a small web app. Effort assumes an existing event/RSVP model; "S" means about a day or less.

| Rank | Feature | Principle | Effort | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"Why are we gathering?" line** on create and on the invite | 1. Purpose | S (one text field plus display) | Foundation for everything else, almost free, and visibly changes how every invite reads. |
| 2 | **One-question RSVP** (host picks a fun question; answers hidden until the event) | 6. Priming | S (one field on the event, one on the RSVP) | The highest-delight item: guests arrive curious, and it feeds features 3 and 5. |
| 3 | **House-rules deck** (pick 1–3 pop-up rules, shown as invite stickers and on arrival) | 5. Pop-up rules | S (static card list plus a custom option) | Gives every event a personality with no AI or backend complexity. |
| 4 | **Morning-after recap** with "Run it back" | 12. After | S–M (scheduled email or push, plus duplicate-event) | Drives retention and sharing, and turns one night into a recurring ritual. |
| 5 | **Reveal the RSVP answers** (group card or "whose answer?" game) | 10. Being seen | S (a reveal toggle on the event page) | Pays off feature 2 and creates laughter in the room. |
| 6 | **Last call and one-word goodbye** | 11. Closing | S–M (timed nudge plus a short-text collector) | Fixes the fizzle-out ending almost no app addresses, and its words feed the recap. |

Close runners-up: the no-maybe RSVP (trivial, and a real help to hosts), group-size dial tiers, and kind-reply templates for +1 requests.

**Suggested build order:** 1 → 2 → 3 (all invite and RSVP surface), then 5 (event-night surface), then 6 → 4 (closing and after). Features 2, 5, 6 and 4 share data, so building them in sequence compounds the effect.
