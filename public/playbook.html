Host playbook

Keeping a small table going

Tips from one host to another, for game nights, build sprints, reading groups and anything with a clear reason to show up. Take what fits.

Say what happens first

  1. Write the first 30 minutes, not the vibe. "We teach Root, then play" or "Ten-minute demos, then we build" tells a careful person exactly what they're walking into. It goes at the top of your invite, above the cover.
  2. Give a range for the group. "6 to 12" is easier to say I'm in to than an open door. People picture the room before they reply.
  3. Put a start and an end on it. A clear end time is a gift. "Leave anytime" and "no speeches" cost you nothing and settle a lot of nerves.
  4. Say if it's quiet. If there's a quieter corner or table, mention it. Some guests only come because it's there.

Type it the way you'd text it: "first 30 min: we teach Root, then play, 6 to 12 people, come solo welcome". Potto fills in the fields.

First-timers

  1. Learn their name before they arrive. It's on your guest list. Saying it at the door does more than any icebreaker.
  2. Give them a small job. Shuffling, setting up the board, picking the first track. Hands busy, nerves down.
  3. Put them next to a regular, not next to another first-timer. Open tables do this for you if you ask a question or two when they reply.
  4. Skip the round of introductions. Introduce people two at a time, with one thing they have in common.
  5. Let them leave early without a fuss. The people who left at 8:30 the first time are often the ones who stay late the third.

No-shows

  1. Remind once, the day before. Potto sends it. One reminder is plenty; three feels like chasing.
  2. Make Can't go easy. People who can say no without awkwardness tell you sooner, and the spot goes to the waitlist.
  3. For 8 or more, plan for one or two missing. Keep a game or task that works with one fewer person.
  4. Start on time anyway. Waiting punishes the people who came.
  5. Don't call anyone out. If someone misses twice, a short private note ("Hope you're OK, the door's open") keeps the door open.

Uneven tables

  1. Have a 3 and a 5 ready. Know one game or format that works for each, so an odd count never stalls the night.
  2. You're the swing player. The host (or a co-host) sits in or sits out to even things up.
  3. Keep pairs together, but not always. Friends who came together stay together the first time. After that, mixing is part of the fun.
  4. Tables of about 4 are where quiet people talk. Bigger than 6 and the loudest voice runs it.

Coming back

  1. Same day, same time, same place. Regulars plan around a rhythm, not an announcement.
  2. Ask once, soon. Within a day, everyone who came gets "Same table next time?" with one tap to say I'm in. No follow-ups.
  3. Watch one number. Your second-RSVP rate: of the people who came, how many replied I'm in again. It's in your event's Guests and After steps.
  4. Small is fine. Six people who come every week are a group. Forty who came once are a list.

Co-hosts

  1. Two hosts don't cancel. When one of you is sick, the night still happens.
  2. Split the jobs. One on the door and names, one on the teach or the build.
  3. Share the credit. Add them under What guests can expect. Their name shows on the invite and in the directory.

The group is yours

Your group's name, roster and history belong to you. Export the roster any time, as a spreadsheet or JSON, from your event's Guests or After step. Each row says whether that guest is OK with emails, texts and showing their name, so you only reach people the way they agreed to.
